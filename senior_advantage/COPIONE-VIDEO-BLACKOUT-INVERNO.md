1 It's nine o'clock on a cold night. The wind picks up, the lights flicker, and then the whole street goes dark. No lamp, no television, no way to charge your phone, and no idea how long it will last. In that moment, everything you do next depends on what is already sitting in your house. Not what you could buy tomorrow, because the roads may be bad and the stores may be dark too. What you have, in the next ten minutes.
2 So here's what's coming. Ten things to have ready before a winter blackout, all taken from official checklists. Some are small, like a flashlight. One is a device that can protect you without making a sound. One has nothing to do with shopping at all, and it may be the most important of the ten. And number seven is a simple thing that tells you whether the food in your refrigerator is safe to eat. Stay until the end, because I'll give you a short plan for this week.
3 Why talk about people over sixty? Because the C D C lists older adults who don't have enough food, clothing or heating among the people hypothermia affects most often. And Ready dot gov has a whole preparedness page written for older adults. That doesn't mean you're fragile. It means a cold house is a bigger problem for some people than for others, so it's smart to plan ahead. And everything in this video comes from official pages: Ready dot gov, the C D C, and the Consumer Product Safety Commission.
4 Now, some of you are thinking, my power hardly ever goes out. Maybe so. But this list doesn't take much time, and a winter blackout can catch you unprepared in a single night. Others are thinking, I have a generator, so I'm covered. Stay for number four, because it's about the one danger a generator can bring into your home. And if you live somewhere warm, don't leave. The same list still helps in any kind of outage.
5 One thing before we start. This isn't medical advice. Where the list touches medicine or medical equipment, your doctor or your pharmacist has the final word, and I'll tell you exactly when. Everything else is what the official pages say, and I'll tell you where each thing comes from. Also, you don't need to buy all ten items in one day. Go through your house first and check what you already own, because you may already have part of this list.
6 Number one. A flashlight for every person in the house, plus extra batteries. According to Ready dot gov, every household member should have a flashlight. And the Consumer Product Safety Commission says to use battery powered flashlights or lanterns instead of candles during a power outage. Keep one where you can reach it from your bed, and one in the kitchen. If you can find it in the dark without standing up and walking around, it's in the right place.
7 Why not candles? That same commission warns that burning candles should never be left unattended, and should be kept away from anything that can catch fire. A candle left alone can turn a blackout into a fire. So if you do light one, never leave the room and leave it burning. But a flashlight is the better answer, which is why it's first on the list. It doesn't need a match, it doesn't flicker, and it can't tip over and start a fire.
8 Number two. A radio that doesn't need the wall outlet. The basic kit on Ready dot gov lists a battery powered or hand crank radio, and a N O A A Weather Radio with tone alert. N O A A Weather Radio is a nationwide network that broadcasts official weather warnings, watches and forecasts twenty four hours a day, seven days a week. When your phone battery is low, a radio can still tell you what's coming. You need a receiver that can pick up that signal, so check the box before you buy.
9 The page for older adults on Ready dot gov also says to keep a weather radio tuned to your local emergency station. And it points to a second tool, the FEMA app, which can send weather alerts for up to five locations in the United States. That could be your home and the town where your family lives. But the app needs a charged phone. So the radio is your backup, and keeping the phone alive is the job of number three.
10 Number three. A way to charge your phone when the outlet is dead. Ready dot gov says to plan for batteries and other alternative power sources, such as a portable charger or a power bank. The basic kit lists a cell phone with chargers and a backup battery. Keep the power bank charged, and charge it again every time you use it. A power bank that's empty in a drawer is just a plastic box. Your phone is your light, your alarm clock and your line to help.
11 And here's a detail to check. Ready dot gov says to find out whether your home phone will work in a power outage, and how long its battery backup will last. Some home phones need electricity and some don't, so you have to look at yours. Check it once, and write the answer on a sticky note. If it doesn't work without power, your cell phone becomes your only phone, and that makes the power bank from number three even more important.
12 Number four. A carbon monoxide alarm, with a battery backup, on every level of your home. Ready dot gov says to install carbon monoxide detectors with battery backup in central locations on every level. The Consumer Product Safety Commission calls carbon monoxide an odorless, colorless and deadly gas. You can't smell it and you can't see it. In a blackout, people sometimes try to heat with a generator, a grill or the stove, and that is exactly when it becomes dangerous.
13 So, three rules from the official pages. A generator goes outdoors only, at least twenty feet from your home, with the exhaust pointed away, and never inside a house, a basement, a shed or a garage. Never heat your home with the stove or the oven. And test your alarms every month, replace the batteries at least once a year, or choose alarms with sealed batteries that last ten years. If an alarm sounds, get outside first, and then call nine one one.
14 Number five. Warm layers you can reach in the dark. The C D C says to keep extra blankets, sleeping bags and winter coats on hand, and the basic kit on Ready dot gov lists a sleeping bag or a warm blanket for each person. Put them together in one spot, like a bin in the hall closet, so you're not digging for them when it's cold and dark. That way, getting warm is one step, not a search through the whole house.
15 Then there are two rules for keeping the heat in. The C D C says to close off rooms you don't need, and it says plainly that turning on the stove for heat is not safe. It also lists the warning signs of hypothermia in adults: shivering, feeling very tired, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness. If you see them in yourself or in someone else, the C D C says this is a medical emergency, so get medical care as soon as you can.
16 Number six. Water and food that don't need power. The basic kit says one gallon of water per person per day for several days, for drinking and for sanitation. And at least a several day supply of non perishable food, with a manual can opener, so a can never becomes a problem. Do the math for your house. If there are two of you, that's two gallons every day. Then multiply by the number of days you want to cover.
17 Check your shelf with that in mind. Food you can eat cold, cans that open without electricity, and water stored somewhere you can reach it. The kit page doesn't list specific foods, so choose things you really like to eat. If a blackout lasts several days, a meal you enjoy matters. And if your doctor has you on a special diet, ask what to keep in the house for those days, because that answer is personal and it isn't mine to give.
18 Number seven. A thermometer for your refrigerator and your freezer. Ready dot gov says that when the power goes out, the refrigerator will keep food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer for about forty eight hours. It also says to keep both closed, and to monitor the temperature with a thermometer. Without a thermometer, you're guessing about your own dinner. With one, you know exactly where you stand.
19 And here is the rule that matters. Ready dot gov says to throw out food if the temperature is forty degrees or higher, and to throw away any food that has been above forty degrees for two hours or more. Its own advice is short: when in doubt, throw it out. If you need more time, it also says to use coolers with ice. Keep the doors shut as much as you can, and check the thermometer instead of the smell.
20 Number eight. A plan for your medicine. The basic kit says to keep prescription medications organized and protected. For a blackout, Ready dot gov adds two more things. Have an alternate plan for refrigerating medicines or for using devices that run on electricity, and find out how long your medicine can be stored at higher temperatures. Those are questions for your doctor or your pharmacist, and the best time to ask is before the storm, not during it.
21 Ready dot gov also says to talk to your medical provider about a power outage plan for any medical device that runs on electricity. And it says that if the power is out for more than a day, any medication that should be refrigerated should be discarded, and you should ask a doctor or pharmacist for a new supply. I'm not telling you what to do with your own medicine. I'm telling you to make that call this week, so you already know your answer.
22 Number nine. A person. The older adults page on Ready dot gov says to create a support network of family, friends and others who can help in an emergency. And it says at least one person in that network should have an extra key to your home, know where your emergency supplies are, and know how to use lifesaving equipment or give your medicine. Think about who that is for you. If you can't think of anyone, that is the first thing to work on.
23 The same page has one more line for anyone who gets regular treatments. If a clinic or a hospital provides them, learn their emergency plans and identify backup providers. So make a short paper list: your support person, your doctor, your pharmacy, and your clinic if you have one. Keep it somewhere you can find it without a phone. Paper never runs out of battery, and in a blackout that matters more than it sounds.
24 Number ten. A warm place to go. This is the one that has nothing to do with shopping. Ready dot gov says to check with local officials about heating and cooling locations open near you, and to go to a community location with power if the cold is extreme. The C D C says that if you can't keep your home warm, you should make temporary arrangements to stay elsewhere. Don't wait for the third cold night to decide.
25 So decide before the storm. Where would you go, how would you get there, and who would take you? The older adults page on Ready dot gov says to plan your transportation in advance if you need help getting out. Write the answer next to the list from number nine. And since the official advice is to check with local officials, find out which places in your town open during an emergency, and how they announce it.
26 Let's recap, because that's ten things. One, a flashlight for every person, with extra batteries. Two, a battery or hand crank radio, and a N O A A Weather Radio. Three, a power bank for your phone. Four, a carbon monoxide alarm on every level. Five, warm layers like blankets, sleeping bags and coats. Six, water and food that need no power. Seven, a thermometer in the refrigerator and freezer. Eight, a plan for your medicine. Nine, a person with a spare key. Ten, a warm place to go.
27 Here is a simple plan for this week. First, go through your house and tick off what you already have. Second, choose the three things you don't have, starting with the carbon monoxide alarm and the flashlights. Third, write the paper list from number nine. And fourth, make the calls to your doctor or pharmacist from number eight. Do those four things, and you've covered the biggest parts of the official lists.
28 One honest note. These are general lists from Ready dot gov, the C D C and the Consumer Product Safety Commission. They don't know your health, your home or your heating system. If you use oxygen, a breathing machine or any medical device that needs power, the plan for it comes from your medical provider. And if an alarm goes off in your home, don't stop to look for the cause. Get outside first, and then call for help.
