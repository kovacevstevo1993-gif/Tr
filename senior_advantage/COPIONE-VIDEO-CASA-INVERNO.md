1 It's January. It's nine degrees outside, and at two in the morning the furnace goes quiet. You're over sixty, you live on a fixed income, and the first thing you think is, I can't afford this. But all across the country, there are people whose job is to help with exactly this moment, and with eight other problems at home this winter. Most seniors never call them. Not because they don't qualify, but because nobody ever told them these people exist.
2 Today I'll show you nine kinds of help that can come to your home this winter if you're over sixty. One of them installs smoke alarms in your house at no charge. One brings a hot meal to your door. One can help protect your heat from being shut off. And number nine is for that night in January, when the furnace stops. Keep it in mind, because it's the most expensive problem on this whole list, and the one people pay for most often without asking.

Most of these work for renters too, and one of them can even help you deal with a landlord who won't fix the heat.

6 Number one. A smoke alarm, installed in your home, at no charge. The American Red Cross runs a program where volunteers come to your house, install smoke alarms for free, and help you make a plan to get out if there's ever a fire. Why does this matter so much after sixty? Because the U S Fire Administration says people sixty five and older are two and a half times more likely to die in a fire than the general population. At eighty five and older, it's more than three times.
7 The Red Cross says working smoke alarms can cut the risk of dying in a home fire by half. Here's the mistake. Many seniors have an alarm on the ceiling that stopped working years ago. Or they took the battery out because it kept beeping, and they can't climb a ladder to fix it. That's exactly who this visit is for. And winter is when it matters most, because that's when the heaters, the blankets and the candles come out, and the windows stay closed.
8 Your action. Go to sound the alarm dot org, type your zip code, and sign up for a home fire safety visit from your local Red Cross. If you don't use the internet, ask a family member or a neighbor to do it with you. Availability depends on where you live, so if there's nothing near you today, check again in a few weeks. And while the volunteers are in your home, ask them to test every alarm you already have, and to show you the fastest way out of each room.
9 Number two. Help with the housework. Through your local Area Agency on Aging, many areas offer what's called homemaker help, for people sixty and older. Someone comes to your home and helps with things like laundry, washing the dishes and light housekeeping. For a lot of people, this small help is the difference between staying in their own home and having to leave it. And most families never hear that it exists until a doctor or a social worker mentions it, often too late.
10 Number three. Help with the heavy jobs. The same offices often have what's called chore help. That's the heavier work, like deep cleaning, hauling things out of the house, or washing the windows. These are exactly the jobs that cause falls, when you try to do them alone on a step stool. What's offered depends on your local agency, so ask exactly what they cover in your area, how often someone can come, and how long the waiting list is right now.
11 So what does this help cost? Here's what the Older Americans Act says. Your local agency can ask some people to share the cost, on a sliding scale based on income. But if your income is at or below the federal poverty line, they can't ask you to share the cost. They can't count your savings, your house or any other property. And if they ask for a donation, you can say no, and they still can't refuse you the service. Most people never hear that part.
12 Number four. A hot meal brought to your door. Meals on Wheels programs bring meals to the homes of adults sixty and older, usually people who find it hard to shop or cook. Meals on Wheels America says these meals may be free, may have a suggested donation, or may be paid on a sliding scale, depending on your area, your income and your need. And for meals paid for by the Older Americans Act, the law does not allow them to make you share the cost.
13 Here's the part most people miss. It's not just the food. Meals on Wheels says local programs may also offer pet food, rides, programs to keep you connected with other people, and even home repairs. And the person who brings the meal also sees how you're doing. In the middle of winter, that knock on the door can matter more than the meal. The mistake is waiting until you can't cook at all. Call while you're still well, and ask what your local program offers.
14 Number five. Grab bars, railings and small repairs. Some Area Agencies on Aging list home repair and grab bar installation among their services. And there's a national nonprofit called Rebuilding Together, with local groups across the country. Many of them say they make repairs at no charge to the homeowner, and they put older adults, people with disabilities and veterans near the top of their list. Who qualifies is based on income, and every local group has its own rules.
15 One of their top priorities for older homeowners is preventing falls. That means grab bars in the bathroom, a second rail on the stairs, a higher toilet and brighter lights. Some local groups also step in during emergencies, when a problem would leave someone without heat, water or electricity. Your action. Search for Rebuilding Together and the name of your city or county, and ask when they take applications, because many of them plan their repair days months ahead.
16 Number six. A lawyer, without a lawyer's bill. Under the Older Americans Act, there is legal help for people sixty and older who need it. The government lists the main problems it covers. Housing, eviction and foreclosure. Fraud and scams that take your money. Guardianship, and the papers that say who decides for you if one day you can't. And here's the key line in the law. Legal assistance is one of the services where they are not allowed to make you share the cost.
17 Think about who this helps. A landlord who won't fix the heat. A contractor who took the deposit and never came back. A phone call that emptied a savings account. Most seniors in these situations do nothing at all, because they think a lawyer costs too much. Your action is to ask your Area Agency on Aging for the legal assistance program in your area. Help goes first to the people with the greatest need, so explain your situation clearly, and bring every paper you have.
18 Number seven. A break for the person who does the caring. If you're caring for a husband, a wife or a parent who is sixty or older, there's a federal program called the National Family Caregiver Support Program. It can give you what's called respite. That means someone takes over for a while, at home or at an adult day center, so you can rest, see your own doctor, or simply sleep through the night. Many caregivers never use it, simply because no one tells them it's there.
19 And here's the part grandparents need to hear. The same program also covers relatives fifty five and older who are raising children under eighteen, like grandparents raising their grandchildren. Besides respite, it can offer counseling, support groups and training. The cost depends on your local program and your income. The mistake is thinking you have to be at the end of your strength before you're allowed to ask. You don't. Ask your Area Agency on Aging about caregiver support.
20 Number eight. Protection from having your heat shut off. Many states have rules that limit when a utility can disconnect you. Some protect homes during certain winter months. Some stop shutoffs on days when it's freezing outside. And some give extra protection to older adults, or to a home where someone is seriously ill. But here's the catch. These rules are not the same everywhere, and they usually don't protect you if you simply stop paying. Many of them require a payment plan that you keep.
21 And the state rules often don't apply to city owned utilities or rural electric co ops. So call your utility before winter, ask what winter protection they offer, and ask if you can be listed as an older or medical customer. If they tell you no, call your state's public utility commission, the office that makes the rules for most utilities. Ask them one question. What protects a senior from a winter shutoff in this state? Write down the name of the person, and the date.
22 Number nine. The furnace that stops in January. Remember the night from the start of this video? Here's the help most people never hear about. The federal heating program, called L I H E A P, allows money to be used for energy related home repairs. And according to the national L I H E A P Clearinghouse, most states offer furnace repair or replacement through the weatherization part of their program. Some states also do it through crisis help, because losing your heat in winter is an emergency.
23 And the U S Department of Energy says its weatherization program gives priority to the elderly, and that renters can apply too. Here's the mistake.
