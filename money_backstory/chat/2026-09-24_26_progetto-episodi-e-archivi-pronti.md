**Corona** (1959-1972): i primi satelliti spia americani, gestiti dalla CIA e nascosti dietro un finto programma scientifico, "Discoverer".

Fotografavano l'Unione Sovietica su pellicola vera. La pellicola scendeva dall'orbita in una capsula col paracadute e un aereo la **afferrava a mezz'aria** con un gancio. Se falliva, la capsula galleggiava ma aveva un **tappo di sale** che in due giorni si scioglieva e la faceva affondare.

Le prime dodici missioni fallirono tutte. Nel 1959 una capsula finì forse sulle **Svalbard** e si sospettò che l'avessero presa i sovietici (ispirò il film *Ice Station Zebra*). Nell'agosto 1960 finalmente funzionò: una sola missione fotografò più territorio sovietico di tutti i voli U-2 messi insieme.

Rimase segreto fino al **1995**, quando Clinton lo declassificò. Oggi quelle foto servono agli archeologi e a chi studia i ghiacciai.

Nota: nell'archivio c'è un errore da non portare nel copione. Discoverer 14 non fu recuperata "il giorno dopo" la 13, ma una settimana dopo (11 e 19 agosto 1960).

**Titolo:** The Plane That Caught a Satellite
**Scritta in miniatura:** CAUGHT FROM SPACE
**Durata stimata:** circa 12 minuti e mezzo (circa 1.450 parole)

## COLD OPEN (clip 1-8): l'aereo che aspetta qualcosa dal cielo

1. August, nineteen sixty. Somewhere over the Pacific, a cargo plane is flying in slow circles… over nothing but empty ocean. Its crew isn't looking for a ship. They're not looking for an enemy.
2. They're waiting for something to fall… out of space.
3. A tiny golden capsule, drifting down under a parachute, from more than a hundred miles up. If they miss it, it will sink to the bottom of the sea — and take America's biggest secret with it.
4. Twice, they miss. On the third pass… they catch it.
5. That same day, seven thousand miles away in Moscow, an American pilot stood in front of a Soviet court — accused of spying from the sky. *[processo Powers]*
6. His name was Francis Gary Powers. He was sentenced to ten years.
7. In Moscow, it looked like America had just lost the spy war. Nobody there knew what was inside that little capsule.
8. And the world wouldn't find out… for thirty-five years.

## BLOCCO 1 (clip 9-16): l'America è cieca

9. To understand why that capsule mattered so much, we have to go back to October, nineteen fifty-seven. The Soviet Union put a beeping metal ball into orbit… and called it Sputnik.
10. Americans looked up — and panicked. If the Soviets could put a satellite over their heads, they could put a bomb there too.
11. Nikita Khrushchev, the Soviet leader, loved to boast. He told the world his factories were turning out missiles… like sausages.
12. Nobody in Washington knew if it was true.
13. The Soviet Union was the biggest country on Earth — and for American intelligence, almost all of it was a blank map.
14. No free press. No open roads. No way to see what was being built behind the Iron Curtain.
15. American spies on the ground could see a street, a factory gate, a train station. Never the whole picture.
16. What Washington needed… was an eye in the sky.

## BLOCCO 2 (clip 17-22): l'U-2

17. For a while, it had one. A strange plane with enormous, glider-like wings, flying higher than any Soviet fighter could climb.
18. It was called the U-2.
19. Every flight came home with photographs of airfields, factories, and missile sites.
20. But every flight was also a gamble. The Soviets tracked them on radar… and they were furious.
21. President Eisenhower was warned: sooner or later, one of those planes would not come home.
22. So, in total secrecy, he approved something far more ambitious. A camera… in orbit.

## BLOCCO 3 (clip 23-30): la copertura "Discoverer"

23. The project was code-named Corona, and it was run by the CIA and the Air Force together.
24. But the public was given a different name: Discoverer. Officially, it was a scientific program — testing satellites, and studying life in space.
25. Newspapers were told the capsules would carry mice… and one day, maybe even monkeys.
26. It was the perfect disguise. Who would suspect a satellite full of mice?
27. The real plan sounded almost insane. The satellite would photograph the Soviet Union from space — on ordinary camera film.
28. Then it would wind that film into a small capsule… and throw it back to Earth.
29. The capsule would fall through the atmosphere, open a parachute over the Pacific — and an Air Force plane would catch it, in mid-air.
30. And if nobody caught it? The capsule would float for a while. Then a plug made of salt would slowly dissolve in the seawater… and it would sink, secrets and all.

## BLOCCO 4 (clip 31-40): un fallimento dopo l'altro

31. The first launch came in early nineteen fifty-nine. And then… everything went wrong.
32. Rockets failed to reach orbit. Cameras jammed. Capsules came down in the wrong place — or never came down at all.
33. One capsule, carrying electronic "mechanical mice," came down near Spitsbergen, in the Arctic. Search teams never found it.
34. Some suspected the Soviets had got there first. The story is said to have inspired a famous Cold War thriller: Ice Station Zebra.
35. Another rocket went so badly off course that it had to be blown up. Pieces rained down on its own launch base — and guards rushed to collect the shattered camera before anyone could see it.
36. Up in orbit, there was an even stranger problem. In the freezing vacuum of space, the film itself kept snapping… like dry spaghetti.
37. A brand-new kind of film had to be developed — tough enough to survive in space.
38. Launch after launch, month after month… nothing came back.
39. Twelve missions in a row. Twelve failures. Rumours spread that the whole program was about to be cancelled.
40. They needed a miracle. Instead… they got a disaster.

## BLOCCO 5 (clip 41-48): primo maggio 1960, l'U-2 abbattuto

41. May first, nineteen sixty. Moscow is celebrating May Day, with soldiers marching through Red Square.
42. Far to the east, over the Ural Mountains, a U-2 is flying at seventy thousand feet. Then — a Soviet missile explodes behind it.
43. The pilot, Francis Gary Powers, gets out, opens his parachute… and is captured alive.
44. Washington, believing the pilot is dead, puts out a cover story: a weather research plane has gone missing.
45. Then Khrushchev springs his trap. He shows the world the wreckage… the spy camera… and the pilot, alive.
46. A summit in Paris, meant to calm the Cold War, collapses in a matter of days.
47. Eisenhower is forced to promise: no more flights over the Soviet Union.
48. America had just lost its only eye over the Soviet Union. Now everything depended on Corona. And Corona had never worked. Not once.

## BLOCCO 6 (clip 49-60): agosto 1960, la cattura

49. Three months later, on the tenth of August, the thirteenth Discoverer lifts off from California.
50. This one carries no camera. It's only a test. A day later, its capsule comes down near Hawaii.
51. The planes miss it… but it's still floating. A Navy team pulls it out of the sea.
52. For the first time in history, human beings have brought something back from orbit.
53. Inside, there's an American flag. Days later, it's handed to President Eisenhower at the White House, in front of the cameras.
54. The whole country celebrates a scientific triumph. Nobody watching knows the real reason for all the smiles.
55. Eight days later, the fourteenth Discoverer goes up. This time, there's a camera on board… and it works.
56. Orbit after orbit, it passes over the Soviet Union, photographing everything beneath it.
57. On August nineteenth, the capsule falls back toward the Pacific. Waiting below is a plane called Pelican Nine, flown by Captain Harold Mitchell.
58. Hooks hang from the back of the plane on long poles, like a giant fishing line. First pass… miss. Second pass… miss again.
59. On the third pass, the hooks snag the parachute — and the capsule is theirs.
60. That same day, in Moscow, Gary Powers was sentenced for spying from a plane. America had just started spying… from space.

## BLOCCO 7 (clip 61-66): cosa c'era sulla pellicola

61. The capsule is flown to the mainland, and the film is rushed to a secret lab to be developed.
62. When the analysts unroll it, they can't believe their eyes. Three thousand feet of film. More than one and a half million square miles of Soviet territory.
63. That one capsule showed more of the Soviet Union than every U-2 flight ever made… put together.
64. The very first picture shows a military airfield at the edge of the Arctic — its runway, a thin white line.
65. By today's standards, the pictures were blurry. But for the first time, America could see… everything.
66. And what it saw… changed everything.

## BLOCCO 8 (clip 67-72): colpo di scena, il "missile gap"

67. That autumn, John F. Kennedy was running for president.
68. One of his sharpest attacks was the "missile gap": the claim that the Soviets had far more nuclear missiles than America.
69. Millions of Americans believed it.
70. But over the next year, Corona searched the Soviet Union from above — base by base, railway by railway.
71. And it found… almost nothing. Instead of hundreds of missiles, the Soviets had only a handful ready to fire.
72. The missile gap was real. It just ran the other way — America was the one far ahead.

## BLOCCO 9 (clip 73-82): la macchina cresce e la capsula in fondo all'oceano

73. After that, Corona became a machine. Bigger planes. Better cameras. Sharper pictures.
74. For twelve years, crews in Hawaii caught capsule after capsule, falling out of the sky.
75. Not every one landed where it should. One came down in Venezuela, where villagers found it… and happily posed for photos with a top-secret spy capsule.
76. By nineteen seventy-two, Corona had flown one hundred and forty-five missions.
77. Its successor was even bigger: a satellite called Hexagon, with far larger capsules.
78. In July, nineteen seventy-one, one of them hit the Pacific when its parachute snapped. It sank more than sixteen thousand feet… to the bottom of the ocean.
79. The CIA refused to give up. The Navy sent a deep-sea submarine, the Trieste Two, which had never gone that deep before.
80. Twice, it failed. On the third dive, in April nineteen seventy-two, its mechanical claw finally grabbed the capsule.
81. Then, on the way back up… the film broke into pieces. After months of searching, and a record-breaking dive, they saved about twenty-five feet.

## FINALE (clip 82-88)

82. For decades, officially, none of this had ever happened. The crews who caught the capsules could never say what was really inside.
83. Then, in February, nineteen ninety-five, President Bill Clinton signed an order… and Corona was finally declassified.
84. More than eight hundred thousand spy photographs were released to the public.
85. Today, those pictures are used for something nobody in nineteen sixty could have imagined. Archaeologists use them to find ancient sites that have since vanished under cities and farmland.
86. Scientists use them to see how glaciers, lakes and coastlines looked sixty years ago.
87. It all began with a cargo plane circling an empty ocean… waiting for a piece of the sky to fall.
88. Those pictures were taken to find missiles. In the end… they became one of the greatest records of our planet ever made.
