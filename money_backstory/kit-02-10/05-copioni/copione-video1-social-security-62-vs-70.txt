Claim Social Security at the wrong age, and it can cost you more than one hundred thousand dollars. Most people think claiming at 62 is a mistake. Others think waiting until 70 is always the smart move. The truth is, both can be wrong. In the next few minutes, I'll show you the exact math, using real numbers from the Social Security Administration, so you can pick the right age for you. And stay until the end, because there's one rule most married couples miss, and it can change everything.
###
We'll answer four questions. How much you really get at 62, 67 and 70. At what age waiting finally pays off. The hidden traps nobody warns you about. And who should claim early, and who should wait.
###
Let's start with the numbers. For anyone born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67. Let's say your full benefit at 67 is two thousand dollars a month. If you claim at 62, you lock in only seventy percent of that. One thousand four hundred dollars a month, for the rest of your life. Now, if you wait past 67, your check grows eight percent for every year you delay. At 70, that same check becomes two thousand four hundred eighty dollars a month. Same worker, same work history. One thousand eighty dollars more, every single month. That's almost thirteen thousand dollars more every year. For many retirees, that's a full year of groceries, plus their Medicare premiums.
###
To see what that really means, meet two neighbors, Frank and Mary. They have the same full benefit, two thousand dollars a month. Frank claims at 62. Mary decides to wait until 70. For eight whole years, Frank collects a check every month, while Mary gets nothing. By the time they both turn 70, Frank has already received about one hundred thirty four thousand dollars. So Frank wins, right? Not so fast.
###
From 70 on, Mary's check is one thousand eighty dollars bigger, every single month. Slowly, she starts catching up. At 75, Frank is still ahead by almost seventy thousand dollars. But around age 80, Mary catches him. This is called the break-even age. At 85, Mary is ahead by sixty thousand dollars. And if they both live to 90, Mary collects about one hundred twenty five thousand dollars more than Frank. Now you might be thinking: what if I die at 72? Then Frank wins, and that's true. That's exactly why this is a personal decision, not a rule. Also, these numbers don't even include the yearly cost of living raises. Those raises are a percentage of your check, so a bigger check gets bigger raises, year after year.
###
So the real question isn't which age is best. It's how long you expect to live, and how much you need the money right now. And here's something most people underestimate. According to the Social Security Administration, a man who reaches 65 today can expect to live, on average, to about 84. A woman, to about 87. And those are only averages. Millions of Americans will live well into their 90s.
###
So far, waiting looks like a clear winner. But before you decide, you need to know the traps. Because some of them can wipe out the advantage of waiting, and one of them can quietly hurt your spouse for decades.
###
Trap number one: claiming early while you're still working. In 2026, if you're under full retirement age and you earn more than twenty four thousand four hundred eighty dollars a year, Social Security holds back one dollar of benefits for every two dollars you earn above that limit. Earn forty thousand dollars, and about seven thousand seven hundred sixty dollars of your checks are withheld. The good news: that money isn't lost forever. Your benefit is recalculated when you reach full retirement age. But many people panic when their checks suddenly stop.
###
Trap number two is the one most married couples miss. When one spouse dies, the survivor doesn't keep both checks. They keep only the larger one. So if the higher earner claims at 62, that reduced check can shrink what the surviving spouse receives for the rest of their life. By waiting, the higher earner isn't just raising their own check. They're protecting the person they love.
###
Trap number three: spousal benefits stop growing at full retirement age. A spouse can receive up to half of the worker's full benefit. But waiting past full retirement age adds nothing to a spousal benefit. So a spouse who will only receive a spousal benefit usually has no reason to wait past full retirement age.
###
Trap number four: taxes. Many retirees are shocked to learn that Social Security can be taxed. If your combined income is above twenty five thousand dollars as a single filer, or thirty two thousand dollars as a married couple, part of your benefits becomes taxable. Up to eighty five percent of your benefits can count as taxable income. And here's the twist: those limits were set decades ago and have never been adjusted for inflation. So every year, more retirees cross them without even noticing.
###
And what if you already claimed, and now you regret it? There are two little known escape doors. First, if you claimed less than twelve months ago, you can withdraw your application. You have to pay back what you received, but then it's as if you never claimed, and your future check can keep growing. Second, once you reach full retirement age, you can ask Social Security to suspend your benefits. Your check stops for a while, but it grows eight percent for every year of suspension, up to age 70.
###
So who should claim early? It can make sense if your health is poor, if you're single and no one depends on your benefit, or if you truly need the income now and have no other savings. Claiming early is not a failure. For some people, it's the smartest choice they can make.
###
And who should wait? If you're healthy, if long life runs in your family, if you're married and you're the higher earner, or if you can live on savings or work income for a few more years, waiting is often the bigger payoff. Remember, every year you wait after 67 adds eight percent to your check, for life. Very few investments can promise that.
